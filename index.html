Xreal's listing filing: market leader, still losing money

TechNode goes through the numbers in Xreal's Hong Kong listing application. Revenue grew 30.8% in 2025 to RMB 516 million and gross margin rose to 35.2% from 22.1%, but the net loss was RMB 456 million, of which RMB 203 million is a fair-value charge on preferred shares and similar instruments. The One series sold about 111,000 units against 17,000 for the older Air line. iResearch data in the filing give Xreal 27% of global revenue in display AR glasses.

Why it matters This is the clearest public look at the economics of display glasses: roughly 128,000 units a year makes a category leader. The figures are TechNode's reading of the filing; the 2027 break-even is the CEO's forecast from May.

A $799, 45 g pair of glasses promises pinch control with no wristband

Hong Kong startup Savage Technology announced its first product, the Orrorin Glasses: full-colour displays for both eyes, eye tracking that fades the image when you look away, and pinch gestures without a ring or band. There is no positional tracking or spatial mapping. A $29 reservation holds a unit, with shipping estimated for March to May 2027, and the pitch is aimed at developers who let AI coding agents write the apps.

Why it matters Camera-free-hand input at this weight would be notable, but the device is still in engineering validation and every figure is the company's own claim.

Deep sea · Genetics

Deep-sea fish and shrimp carry genetic signs of extinction risk

A Florida International University team led by Pedro Peres sequenced DNA from 486 specimens of three lanternfish and two deep-sea shrimp species, collected in the Gulf of Mexico between 2011 and 2023. All five showed traits linked to higher extinction risk, including small effective population sizes. One lanternfish, Diaphus dumerilii, lost about 8% of its genetic diversity in twelve years. Habitat models project losses of up to 70% by 2050 and 2100 for four of the five species.

Why it matters Lanternfish are among the most abundant vertebrates on Earth, so abundance is evidently no guarantee of genetic health. The authors did not tie the changes to the 2010 Deepwater Horizon spill, and some populations may already have been vulnerable. Read from the press summary, not the paper.

Biology · Materials

The narwhal's spiral tusk is built from two helices that twist opposite ways

A team led from Aarhus University, with first author Adrian Rodriguez-Palomo, mapped the orientation of mineralised collagen through narwhal tusks using X-ray tensor tomography at three European synchrotrons. The visible spiral turns out to be assembled from microscopic building blocks with opposing twists, and the thin outer cementum layer forms a left-handed helix. The pattern appeared only when each point's orientation was compared with its neighbours.

Why it matters A structural answer to how a tooth up to 2 m long grows straight while spiralling. The press release gives no count of tusks scanned and says what the tusk is for remains uncertain.

Ecology · Feature

Whales are returning to Argentine Patagonia in record counts

A Mongabay feature reports that the 2025–26 season logged 3,410 southern right whales, 2,600 sei whales and 250 humpbacks, which it calls a record. A Beagle Channel photo-identification project has catalogued 208 individual humpbacks since 2013. Two satellite-tagged sei whales in the San Jorge Gulf suggest the animals feed there from October to June before heading north toward Brazil.

Why it matters Recovery is meeting new pressure from krill fishing, ship traffic and oil and gas expansion in the San Matías Gulf. These are monitoring figures from a news feature, not a peer-reviewed analysis, and nothing is said about changes in survey effort.

Conference

Marine biodiversity meeting: 2,000 new species a year, and too few taxonomists

Mongabay's report from the 7th World Conference on Marine Biodiversity (Bruges, 17–20 November, more than 800 attendees) centres on how much remains undescribed. The World Register of Marine Species passes 250,000 known species and grows by about 2,000 a year, against estimates of 500,000 to 2 million in total. Speakers described a crisis in taxonomic expertise, and a Ghent researcher presented nematode diversity before and after a 2021 mining trial in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone.

Why it matters It echoes yesterday's Flemish Cap barcoding result: sequencing cannot name what nobody has described. The mining sessions were talks, not published findings.

arXiv · Input

MidasTUI: any object at hand becomes a tangible controller

arXiv had posted no new listing when I checked, so these come from Thursday's batch. Ying, Chen, Cheng and a colleague use a wearable display's camera and a Transformer-based temporal classifier to detect how a hand is touching an everyday object, then infer which control it should act as: slider, mouse or keypad. They report that recognition generalises across objects and users. In a comparative study, participants rated it less physically tiring and more usable than mid-air gestures.

Why it matters Arm fatigue is the standing complaint about glasses input, and this needs no extra hardware. The listing abstract gives no sample size or effect sizes, and no venue.

arXiv 2611.34945
arXiv · AI and wearables

GlassGround: interface-reading models lose half their accuracy through a worn camera

Wang, Sun, Du and colleagues built a benchmark of 3,250 tasks pairing phone screenshots with photos of the same screens taken by four wearable devices under four viewing conditions. Across ten open-source models, accuracy at locating the right on-screen element fell from 94.8% on screenshots to 48.5% on camera images. Their training method plus local zooming recovers part of the gap, reaching 69.6% and 64.4% on two models.

Why it matters Assistants in glasses are expected to help with the screens you are looking at; this puts a number on how far that is from working. No human participants; the devices are not named in the abstract.

arXiv 2611.33760
arXiv · AR and vehicles

What pedestrians want from AR when talking to driverless cars

Clérigo, Schrapel, Rito and colleagues combine a literature synthesis, a survey of 124 people and a working prototype of two-way interaction using AR, speech and gestures. Respondents preferred AR cues that make a vehicle's motion or warnings explicit, simple voice commands and conventional gestures. Prototype tests showed a trade-off: processing on the device is faster, processing with roadside infrastructure is more accurate.

Why it matters Most work on this problem puts displays on the car; this puts them on the pedestrian. Preferences are stated in a survey, not observed in traffic.

arXiv 2611.33581
arXiv · VR and robotics

RoboJackVR: a VR game whose players train robots

Ma and Cai package robot tasks as a standalone VR game, with the physics simulation running on the headset itself, so that play produces demonstration data. Policies trained only on those demonstrations kept about 91% of the baseline success rate across 66 benchmark tasks. The abstract reports no player study, so whether anyone would play it is open.
